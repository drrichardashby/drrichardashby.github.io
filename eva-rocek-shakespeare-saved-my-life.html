Eva Rocek: Shakespeare Saved My Life

7 September 2026

As I work on revisions to my new book, Shakespeare and Holocaust Writing, I thought I would post about some of the writers and Shakespearean allusions I look at.

I want to begin with Eva Rocek (1927–2015).

Rocek was a Czech Jewish survivor of Terezín (Theresienstadt), Auschwitz-Birkenau and Kurzbach, a subcamp of Gross-Rosen for women. She gave her memoir the title Shakespeare Saved My Life.

‘I am a storyteller, not a writer’, Rocek tells us at its outset.

Before her deportation, Rocek had been cast as Titania in a production of Dream at the Jewish Orphanage in Prague. She memorised the part, although the production never took place: she was deported to Terezín before it could be performed.

Later, at Kurzbach, Rocek was assigned to work as a gravedigger. On the way to and from the burial ground, she entertained the other prisoners by reciting poetry, including Dream.

One of the guards appeared to listen. Rocek calls him ‘Mr Suchy’, although she knew neither his identity nor his background for certain; it was rumoured that he came from the German-speaking Sudetenland and had a Czech name.

One day, he addressed her using the formal Sie—highly unusual, and against the rules governing contact between guards and prisoners—and asked: ‘Was haben Sie studiert?’ (‘What have you studied?’). Rocek replied that she was only a high-school student. He then asked the profession of her father. She told him that he was a lawyer.

When Kurzbach was evacuated, Suchy intervened to stop the sick prisoners from being taken to a mass grave. Later, he secretly warned Eva and her mother: ‘Do not go to Gross-Rosen!’ Eva, her mother and other women subsequently slipped out of the marching column and escaped into the forest.

It is a remarkable story and should be uplifting. Shakespeare forms a bridge between perpetrator and victim: Rocek reflects that Suchy, hearing her quote the ‘universal’ words of Shakespeare, recognized her shared ‘humanity’ and facilitated her escape.

But the story is also problematic.

Suchy does not recognise a ‘universal’ humanity. He recognises the humanity of Rocek because she is educated, cultivated, and the daughter of a lawyer. Shakespeare is not simply a bridge across the ideological division between human and nonhuman. He also risks becoming a cultural credential—a sort of password by which one prisoner becomes legible as fully human.

The story leaves us with an uncomfortable question. What happens to those who cannot produce the approved signs of culture, education and humanity?

My book is concerned with the ways writers use Shakespeare to interrogate humanist traditions after Auschwitz—and to begin imagining new humanisms in its wake. I show that in Rocek Shakespeare can disclose a common humanity that racist ideology denies; but using Shakespeare to guarantee ‘the human’ may also reinforce the division between human and non-human.

Rocek emigrated to the United States in 1960 and taught chemistry at the University of Illinois Chicago. Her husband Jan, whom she met in Terezín, survived Terezín, Auschwitz, Meuselwitz and the death march that followed its evacuation. At 101, he still talks to students about his experiences.
