‘Hamlet Is Not Worth Dying For’

Jiří Weil, Shakespeare, and survival in Nazi-occupied Prague

Life with a Star depicts an actor who risks his life to perform Hamlet. The episode asks a painful question: is survival always the highest human value, or can ideas, desires, and our forms of life mean more than life itself?

Life with a Star, the great 1949 Holocaust novel by Czech writer and survivor Jiří Weil, follows Josef Roubíček, a Jewish former bank clerk living under increasingly brutal restrictions in Nazi-occupied Prague. As the persecution intensifies, Roubíček finds that his world steadily contracts around the demands of survival: food, work, permits, avoiding deportation, and the struggle to remain alive, at all costs.

One of the most striking minor characters in the novel is Ludvík Porges, an actor whom Roubíček encounters while undertaking compulsory labour.

Porges had spent his theatrical career playing comic parts. Bald and ‘barrel-shaped’, Porges tells Roubíček that nobody would let him play the great tragic roles he wanted: Hamlet or King Lear. ‘Who ever heard of a bald Hamlet with a beer belly’, he asks, ‘or a King Lear who looks like a fattened-up drunk?’

Under Nazi occupation, however, Porges finds himself forced into precisely the tragic ‘role’ the theatre had denied him. He must now, as he puts it, play his tragic part under his ‘own name’. He would prefer to go back to playing Falstaff.

Yet Porges continues to act.

Later in the novel, Roubíček learns that Porges has been arrested after curfew. He possesses a permit, but it is valid for another street. He has taken the risk because he has been visiting a family who give him tea and for whom he performs Hamlet – the tragic part he always wanted.

The reaction of another forced labourer is brutally simple: ‘Hamlet is not worth dying for’.

If Porges had risked his life to obtain pork, that might at least have made sense; it would have been a risk to his life, but it might ultimately have contributed to his survival.

But to die for Hamlet? What is the point of that?

The remark appears almost impossible to dispute. Food means life; a permit means life. Hamlet, however, means more or less nothing. Placed against the demands of self-preservation, playing Hamlet to a few other people in a cramped front room is completely irrational, absurd.

But – is it?

The question is not simply whether Hamlet is worth dying for. Nor is it whether Shakespeare represents an absolute cultural value, which must be preserved whatever the costs. What the novel asks is whether survival is worth it, if staying alive means abandoning human desire, culture, artistry, and values altogether. If survival is the only value, human life becomes absurd, ‘without meaning’. Roubíček wonders if Porges, by dying ‘for reciting Hamlet, not because he has some tasty meat’, has made his death a ‘sacrifice’, and might even be thought of as a ‘martyr’.

Weil is not a romantic sentimentalist. He does not finally tell us that Hamlet is worth dying for. Nor does he turn Porges into an uncomplicated martyr for European culture. Hamlet is clearly not worth dying for: that conclusion retains its terrible force. But Porges shows everything that is lost when survival becomes the only possible value. To live merely in order to continue living is precisely the reduction Roubíček fears when he says that the persecuted have been ‘reduced to animals’. When he undertakes his performance as Hamlet – unnecessary, impractical, and dangerous as it is – Porges asserts a desire for something beyond life for its own sake: culture, vocation, relationships with others, self-realisation, meaning.

The real horror is not that Porges chooses Hamlet over survival. It is that Nazi persecution creates a world in which life and meaning have been forced into opposition. Weil leaves us with the question unresolved. Hamlet may not be worth dying for. Weil asks whether survival alone is enough to live for.

Weil certainly knew the stakes: to avoid deportation to the camps, he faked his own suicide. To survive, he had to disappear, to cut himself off from a world that gave his life its meaning. But survive he did.

Sources

Jiří Weil, Life with a Star, trans. Rita Klímová, with Roslyn Schloss (London: Daunt Books, 2012).
