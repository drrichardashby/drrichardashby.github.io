Shakespeare in the Łódź Ghetto

Avraham Lasky and Hamlet at the limits of European humanism

A multilingual diary written in the margins of a French novel records how one young man in the Łódź Ghetto turned to Hamlet as he confronted the possible destruction of the Ghetto and of European culture itself.

One of the most unusual and striking Shakespearean documents to survive the Holocaust is the so-called Les Vrais Riches diary, which was discovered in the ruins of the Łódź Ghetto. Its author did not name himself, but Yad Vashem has since identified him as Avraham Lasky.

Lasky wrote his diary in the margins of the 1882 novel Les Vrais Riches by the French author, François Coppée. Lasky was clearly highly educated: his diary moves between Yiddish, Hebrew, Polish, and English, while at times he also quotes German and French. The multilingualism of the diary records the meeting of Western and Eastern languages and cultures in interwar Poland. It also reveals the cosmopolitan intellectual world that the Nazis sought to destroy.

Hamlet in the margins

Shakespeare holds an important place in the Les Vrais Riches diary. Lasky quotes Hamlet more than once and records his ‘pride’ in understanding Shakespeare in the original English. Lasky does not appeal to Shakespeare for consolation, however. Hamlet enters the diary under the pressure of immediate danger. On 19 July 1943, Lasky wonders when the question ‘to be or not to be’ might be ‘taken off our shoulders’. Lasky transforms a soliloquy about suicide to contemplate the collective fate of the Ghetto – perhaps even the Jews as a whole. ‘To be’ names a distant hope of survival; ‘not to be’ the prospect of genocidal destruction.

Lasky returns to the soliloquy a few days after, when he writes of ‘the rub’ – the consideration – on which his life now hangs: whether Hitler will be defeated before the Ghetto is finally dissolved. Once again, Lasky uses Hamlet to chronicle his uncertainty, his tension, as he worries at the question of survival.

This is Shakespearean quotation as testimony. Lasky does not merely display his learning – prolific as it is. He turns to Hamlet to record both his own anxiety and that of an entire, persecuted population, as it awaits its fate. The words remain instantly recognizable – but the scale and meaning have changed radically.

Perhaps it is the especially fraught conditions of Łódź – the longest-lasting Nazi Ghetto in the East – that meant other diarists interned there also quoted Hamlet in 1944. The Yiddish diarist Menahem Oppenheim and the Austrian-born writer Oskar Rosenfeld both turn to Hamlet and ‘To be or not to be’. Writing in his final diary entries over the period 15th-17th August, Oppenheim reflects that ‘I am writing these words but God knows for whom. The ground [is] ever hotter. The culmination point of to be or not to be comes closer from day to day’. Rosenfeld, playwright and cofounder of Jewish Stage Theatre in Vienna, also used Hamlet to describe the uncertainties of life and death in the Ghetto in its final stages, where obtaining soup ‘is a question of “to be or not to be”’.

The failure of European culture

Lasky also names Homer, Dante, and Goethe. His diary is steeped in the canonical literature of Europe. But his attachment to that tradition is inseparable from his disgust at the depths to which ‘civilized’ Europe has plunged. He writes of his ‘revulsion’ towards our ‘accursed European civilization’ and its claims to ‘morality’. Lasky turns to Shakespeare to testify to the last days of Łódź. But Shakespeare is also made to answer whether European culture can answer to the Ghetto at all.

Lasky asks whether he will be able to tell humanity about everything he has witnessed: ‘I dream about telling to humanity but should I be able?’ He goes on to sharpen the question: ‘Should Shakespeare be able?’ It is a startling moment. Shakespeare conventionally stands for unrestrained eloquence, the writer who could ‘body forth’ anything. Lasky, however, raises the possibility that events have overtaken Shakespeare, outstripping even his prodigious imaginative capacities. If Shakespeare cannot find the words, the failure is not only literary: it belongs to a culture that has proved unable to prevent persecution and genocide. The literary canon has no answer for Auschwitz. ‘No bridge led from death in Auschwitz to Death in Venice’, as Jean Améry once remarked.

Hamlet provides Lasky with a language for fear, suspended judgement, and threatened existence. But at the same time, Lasky asks whether Shakespeare still has any purchase on a world marked by previously unimagined death and depravity.

Cosmopolitanism and a Jewish future

Facing the collapse of European culture, Lasky reflects more than once on whether he will emigrate to Palestine, in the unlikely event he does escape or survive Łódź:

My mind is still further disordered. In this [these] infernal conditions in which I am plunged, I trouble my mind and cannot decide if... o! fool that I am! – I shall go to Palestine or remain where I am. I want to go, Socialist cosmopolitist, and I still have many misgivings in nearest realisation of the World United States; and still, dear old Hebrew and ancient Palestine has an irressisstible [sic.] fascination for me — alt[h]ough in my terrible wrath against nationalism caused by the barbaric German exaggeration — I rebuke myself as being particularist, ‘a parochialist’ – but after all I scarcely believe that, let us say, the Poles would over night [sic.] forget their age long hate towards the […] Jews.

On the one hand, Lasky imagines himself emigrating to Palestine, where he will speak ‘dear old Hebrew’, which he also describes elsewhere in his diary as ‘the language of the future’, while Yiddish is ‘the mother tongue’. On the other, he is also wary of ‘particularism’, ‘parochialism’, and ‘nationalism’, as embodied by Nazism, and cleaves to a more egalitarian cosmopolitan worldview, even while he is also suspicious of ‘the World United States’. Lasky grapples with the universalist values of the European cultural past (and the American future) and his desire to preserve Jewish history, tradition, and language against genocide.

His knowledge of European literary culture and his Jewishness are by no means inherently at odds. But when Lasky considers his desire to emigrate to Palestine, he may be leaving a redundant, universal humanist culture in his wake, as he embraces a more self-consciously Jewish cultural and linguistic identity.

Not unlike ‘Shakespeare Saved My Life’, Les Vrais Riches resists an easy story about the consoling power of literature in times and places of extremity. Hamlet survives in the margins as a language through which Lasky might describe the uncertain fate of the ghetto and the Jews of Europe. But Lasky also ponders the failure of ‘civilized’ European culture. His final challenge remains unresolved: does Shakespeare still speak to us after the Holocaust, or has the canon been rendered obsolete?

The Les Vrais Riches diary is held at Yad Vashem, but there are English translations available. Excerpts from the diary appear in Lodz Ghetto: Inside a Community Under Siege, while a fuller (though still not entirely complete) English translation also appears in Salvaged Pages: Young Writers’ Diaries of the Holocaust. Excitingly, the British artist and writer Tom de Freston is currently completing a graphic novel adaptation of the diary. I have been lucky enough to see drafts as Tom works on the diary. It is a truly remarkable piece of work, which will bring more attention to Lasky and his powerful testament.
