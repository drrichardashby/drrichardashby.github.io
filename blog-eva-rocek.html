Eva Rocek: Shakespeare Saved My Life

Shakespeare, Holocaust testimony, and an uncomfortable question about culture and the human.

As I work on revisions to my new book, Shakespeare and Holocaust Writing, I thought I would post about some of the writers and Shakespearean allusions I look at.

I want to begin with Eva Rocek (1927–2015).

Rocek was a Czech Jewish survivor of Terezín (Theresienstadt), Auschwitz-Birkenau and Kurzbach, a subcamp of Gross-Rosen for women. She gave her memoir the title Shakespeare Saved My Life.

‘I am a storyteller, not a writer’, Rocek tells us at its outset.

Before her deportation, Rocek had been cast as Titania in a production of A Midsummer Night’s Dream at the Jewish Orphanage in Prague. She memorised the part, although the production never took place: she was deported to Terezín before it could be performed.

Later, at Kurzbach, Rocek was assigned to work as a gravedigger. On the way to and from the burial ground, she entertained and consoled the other prisoners by reciting poetry, including Dream.

One of the guards appeared to listen. Rocek calls him ‘Mr Suchy’, although she knew neither his identity nor his background for certain; it was rumoured that he came from the German-speaking Sudetenland and had a Czech name.

One day, he addressed her using the formal Sie—highly unusual, and against the rules governing contact between guards and prisoners—and asked: ‘Was haben Sie studiert?’ (‘What have you studied?’). Rocek replied that she was a high school student, and the daughter of a lawyer.

When Kurzbach was evacuated, Suchy intervened to stop the sick prisoners from being taken to a mass grave. Later, he secretly warned Eva and her mother: ‘Do not go to Gross-Rosen!’ Eva, her mother and other women subsequently slipped out of the marching column and escaped into the forest.

It is a remarkable story and should be uplifting. Shakespeare forms a bridge between perpetrator and victim: Rocek reflects that Suchy, hearing her quote the ‘universal’ words of Shakespeare, recognised her shared ‘humanity’ and eventually facilitated her escape.

But the story is also problematic.

Shakespeare is not simply the site of a shared humanity. Through Shakespeare, Suchy recognises a very specific form of humanity — the humanity posited by the tenets of the European humanist tradition, in which Shakespeare occupies a privileged place. Rocek is cultivated, ‘studied’, dignified, bourgeois. Shakespeare becomes a form of cultural and even racial capital—a means by which a Jewish prisoner becomes legible as fully human.

The story leaves us with an uncomfortable question. What happens to those who cannot produce the approved signs of culture, education, and humanity?

My book is concerned with the ways writers use Shakespeare to interrogate humanist traditions after Auschwitz—and to begin imagining new humanisms in its wake. I show that in Rocek Shakespeare can disclose a common humanity that racist ideology denies; but when Shakespeare becomes a guarantor of ‘the human’, it may also serve to reinforce the very division between human and nonhuman on which that ideology depends.

Rocek emigrated to the United States in 1960 and taught chemistry at the University of Illinois Chicago. Her husband Jan, whom she met in Terezín, survived Terezín, Auschwitz, Meuselwitz and the death march that followed its evacuation. At 101, he still talks to students about his experiences.
